Extremality shift of rotating black holes
at finite Gauss–Bonnet coupling
Abstract: We study how the minimum mass of a rotating black hole at fixed angular momentum changes
with the Gauss–Bonnet coupling in five dimensions. We construct stationary, asymptotically flat solutions
with two equal angular momenta at finite coupling and calculate the thermodynamic potential Ψ conjugate
to that coupling. The calculation differentiates the discretised field equations implicitly and solves a linear
response problem; it avoids real finite differences between neighbouring solutions, but remains numerical.
Extrapolating to zero temperature gives the local slope ∂Mext /∂α|J , subject to a regular extremal limit.
Across the sampled range this slope stays positive: it decreases from the known perturbative value π to
about 1.07149, then rises to 1.44440. Thus the extremal mass increases monotonically while its slope does
not. The potential and the changes in the extrapolated mass curve satisfy an integral consistency check,
with shared numerical inputs and errors. We validate the calculation against the analytic static solution,
the published perturbative potential and the extremal near-horizon entropy. We report sensitivity to the
extrapolation model, fitting window and temperature coordinate, and restrict the interpretation to classical
Einstein–Gauss–Bonnet theory in the range computed.

Keywords: Black Holes, Classical Theories of Gravity, Higher Curvature Gravity

1    Introduction
For a rotating black hole, extremality marks the limiting mass at a given angular momentum
on the branch under consideration. Changing the gravitational theory changes that limit. The

                                                    –1–
question addressed here is quantitative: how does the extremal mass respond when the coefficient
of a Gauss–Bonnet term is increased, beyond an infinitesimal perturbation of Einstein gravity?
    We study five-dimensional, asymptotically flat black holes with two equal angular momenta,
J1 = J2 = J. The equality of the spins reduces the metric equations to ordinary differential
equations in the radial coordinate. At zero coupling the solution is Myers–Perry [5]. For a
Gauss–Bonnet term with the normalisation used below, the extremal mass is known to first order
[8]:
                                 Mext = 23 π 1/3 J 2/3 + πα + O(α2 ).                             (1)

Here J is the angular momentum in each plane. The initial slope is positive, but this expression
does not determine the slope at finite coupling. The perturbative thermodynamics can be ob-
tained without the corrected metric using the method of Reall and Santos [4]; relevant results for
Myers–Perry black holes and their metric corrections are given in [6, 7].
   The underlying finite-coupling solutions are also known numerically. Brihaye, Kleihaus, Kunz
and Radu [9] constructed the equal-spin family and derived its extremal near-horizon entropy
using Sen’s entropy function [10]. Extremal bulk solutions and their domain of existence were
studied further in [11]. Our focus is the response of this family in the coupling direction, including
the finite-coupling dependence of the extremal mass slope.
   We use the extended first law

                                  dM = T dS + 2ΩH dJ + Ψ dα.                                      (2)

The quantity Ψ is a thermodynamic response coefficient for a whole solution, not an additional
radial field. It measures the change of mass per unit change of coupling at fixed entropy and
angular momenta. Extended first laws for Lovelock couplings are developed in [12, 20]; the latter
explicitly treats stationary black holes. Varying α compares equilibrium solutions of theories
with different couplings. It does not describe a time-dependent change of the coupling in a single
spacetime.
   The computation has three steps. We first solve the nonlinear radial boundary-value problem
at fixed horizon radius, angular velocity and coupling. We then differentiate that discretised
problem to determine how its solution and charges respond to α, and use (2) to extract Ψ.
Finally, we follow the family towards zero temperature and extrapolate Ψ. On a regular extremal
branch the first law identifies that limit with ∂Mext /∂α|J . The thermodynamic identities are
analytic; the rotating finite-coupling solutions, their responses and the extremal extrapolation
are numerical.
   The measured potential changes sign between slowly rotating and near-extremal states. Its
extremal limit stays positive over the sampled couplings, falls substantially below π, and then
rises again. The result concerns the slope of the mass, not a non-monotonic mass. We check this
interpretation against changes in the extrapolated mass curve, while accounting for the numerical
inputs shared by the two calculations.
   Extremality shifts are often discussed in connection with the weak gravity conjecture [1, 2] and
the thermodynamic entropy–extremality relation of Goon and Penco [3]. For the present rotating,
uncharged family we use that relation as a thermodynamic identity, without identifying angular
momentum with electric charge. Our finite-coupling result is a statement about classical Einstein–
Gauss–Bonnet gravity. Its interpretation as a gravitational effective field theory is restricted, as
discussed in section 6.
   Section 2 specifies the solutions and observables. Section 3 explains the potential and its
numerical evaluation. Results and checks follow in sections 4 and 5; appendix A describes the
numerical construction and the sensitivity of the extrapolation.

3     What the Gauss–Bonnet potential measures
3.1   Definition and evaluation along the numerical family
By definition, Ψ is the derivative of mass with respect to α at fixed entropy and both angular
momenta. The solver, however, fixes rH and ΩH . Along its coupling direction, both entropy and
angular momentum generally change. Evaluating the first law on that direction gives

                       ∂M       ∂M           ∂S              ∂J
                  Ψ=          =           −T           − 2ΩH           .                      (11)
                       ∂α S,J   ∂α rH ,ΩH    ∂α rH ,ΩH       ∂α rH ,ΩH

The subtraction removes the mass changes associated with entropy and spin, leaving the response
to the coupling itself. Equation (11) is therefore valid for the rotating stationary solutions, not
only for the static member. It does not equate partial derivatives with different constraints:
it equates one constrained derivative to a combination of three derivatives evaluated along a
different path.
   No terms such as S ∂α T appear, because we evaluate the differential first law; we do not
differentiate a putative expression M = T S + 2ΩH J. More generally any differentiable path
through equilibrium solutions with dα ̸= 0 gives the same Ψ after subtracting the entropy and
spin contributions and dividing by dα.

Suppose the sampled family approaches a regular extremal branch with S = Sext (J, α). Differen-
tiating Mext (J, α) = M (Sext (J, α), J, α) gives

                           ∂Mext    ∂Sext

To infer this value from non-extremal numerical solutions we assume that their potentials have
a continuous limit on that branch and that the entropy term has a vanishing limit. A bounded
extremal entropy derivative is a sufficient regularity condition. Agreement with the algebraic
near-horizon entropy and smooth sampled data supports this assumption over the range studied,
but a finite table is not a proof of it.

Where the Jacobian A is invertible, one linear solve gives the response of the discretised metric.
Propagating it through the same charge and entropy functionals gives the three derivatives in
(11). The entropy response retains the explicit coupling derivative of (7), in addition to the
change of the horizon geometry.
   The production code assembles A by the chain rule from local complex-step derivatives of the
radial equations and horizon relations. The parameter derivative on the right is also evaluated
by complex step. For a real analytic function, Im f (p + iϵ)/ϵ = f ′ (p) + O(ϵ2 ). A small imaginary
step avoids the subtraction of nearly equal real numbers. The implementation and its verification
are described in appendix A.
   Equation (13) is an analytic differentiation identity for the discrete system. Its numerical
solution still carries discretisation, nonlinear-solver, linear-solver and rounding errors. The ex-
tracted observables also carry asymptotic fitting errors. We consequently refer to this procedure
as implicit numerical response, rather than an exact finite-coupling determination of Ψ.
   This local linear response is evaluated about solutions at finite α. It does not truncate the
whole family at first order about α = 0. That distinction is what allows us to measure a non-
perturbative dependence on the coupling within classical EGB gravity.
   As a check, we compare the response with central real differences of neighbouring solutions.
At 4 points, using 5 step sizes related by halving, the discrepancy decreases by factors between
3.951 and 4.028, consistent with second-order convergence. The largest initial gap is 1.47 ×
10−2 . Responses in both α and ΩH also provide tangent predictors for the continuation towards
extremality.


4     Results
The dataset contains 356 accepted solutions at 13 couplings from α = 0 to α = 1/2 in units
rH = 1. It reaches x = 0.5001 on the sampled family and x = 0.4170 on the extrapolated
extremal branch. These are subsets of the allowed domain, whose published bound is x < 1 [11].
We follow the branch continuously connected to Myers–Perry. Continuation and regularity checks
identify the branch sampled here; they do not establish global uniqueness or exhaust its domain.

6     Discussion
The observable calculated here is the response of mass to the Gauss–Bonnet coupling at fixed
entropy and angular momenta. Its zero-temperature limit gives the response of the extremal
mass at fixed angular momenta, provided the branch has a regular thermodynamic limit. The
numerical evidence supports a positive, non-monotonic slope over the sampled finite-coupling
range. The positive initial slope was already known; the substantial decrease and subsequent
increase are the finite-coupling behaviour investigated here.
   The distinction between a slope and an accumulated mass change matters. At fixed J, the
mass keeps increasing in the range measured. Its rate of increase reaches a lowest sampled value
near α = 0.15 in units rH = 1, corresponding to x = 0.1669, and then recovers. By x = 0.0871
the slope is 1.3260, below half the perturbative value. The data do not establish its sign or shape
outside this interval.
   Positive Ψ also implies that entropy decreases with α at fixed (M, J) for positive-temperature
states in that region. Meanwhile the entropy per spin along the extremal branch grows by a factor
4.8 in the sampled range. There is no contradiction: extremal states at fixed J have different
masses as α changes.
   There is another constraint distinction at low spin. Holding the coordinate radius rH fixed
does not hold the entropy fixed. At α = 1/2 the measured mass initially decreases slightly with
spin before increasing. The first law permits this when the negative entropy contribution exceeds
the rotational contribution. The parity of S(q) and J(q) does not determine the sign of ∂q M . We
therefore do not interpret a mass below the static value at the same rH as an error or use that
static value as a lower bound on the rotating family. The published bounds x < 1 and j ≤ 1 [11]
are different statements: our largest scaled spin is 1.000000, and the extremal value decreases to
0.66328 at the largest sampled coupling.

                                                             – 10 –
   The calculation treats EGB as a classical theory with its specified action. Second-order equa-
tions do not by themselves establish existence, uniqueness, stability or a well-posed problem at
every coupling. We make claims only for the constructed branch and its extrapolated endpoints.
In a gravitational effective field theory, terms beyond Gauss–Bonnet generically contribute at
the same order as the higher powers of α retained by this finite-coupling calculation. Its non-
monotonicity is consequently not a universal prediction of a derivative expansion. Causality
restrictions on higher-curvature graviton interactions provide an additional reason to distinguish
the classical truncation from an ultraviolet completion [19].
   The main limitations are the extremal extrapolation, the incomplete coupling coverage and
the equal-spin restriction. The displayed bands measure sensitivity to specified fitting choices;
they are not rigorous numerical error bounds. The mass-curve consistency check shares systematic
errors with the potential calculation, while the near-horizon check determines entropy rather than
mass. The near-horizon geometry discards the asymptotic normalisation needed to recover the
global mass by itself. A direct construction of extremal solutions with response measurements,
additional resolution studies, and a comparison with an independent solver would strengthen the
quantitative determination. A second-order perturbative calculation would also provide a useful
test of the initial departure from Ψext = π.


A     Numerical construction and sensitivity analysis
Radial discretisation. We set rH = 1, introduce z = 1/r and collocate in χ = 1 − z. The
horizon is at χ = 0 and infinity at χ = 1. The unknown amplitudes factor out the boundary
behaviour:
                                                             1 + z4H
               b = (1 − z 2 )B,    f = (1 − z 2 )F,     h=           ,    w = z 4 W.
                                                                z2
They are represented at Chebyshev–Lobatto nodes. At infinity we impose B = F = 1, H ′ = W ′ =
0. At the horizon, where the solved interior equations are singular, we impose three regularity
relations and W (0) = ΩH . The equations and horizon relations are generated symbolically.
Damped Newton iteration solves the collocation system. Production walks use N = 64, with
N = 96 available when acceptance requires refinement; N = 48 is used for selected comparison
walks.

Jacobian and response. The production Newton and response solves use the same chain-rule
assembly of ∂RN /∂u. Local partial derivatives are computed by complex step: seven vectorised
partials of the interior equations and nine of the horizon relations. The coupling and angular-
velocity derivatives are computed by complex step in the respective parameter, with ϵ = 10−20 .
The rational residual is evaluated using complex arithmetic without non-analytic operations on
the perturbed variables. The assembled Jacobian is checked entrywise against a dense complex-
step perturbation of every nodal unknown, including profiles that do not solve the equations.
This checks the chain-rule assembly; both implementations still use complex-step differentiation.

Continuation. Starting from Myers–Perry, we continue in coupling and spin. The tangent
predictor u + ∆α ∂α u + ∆q ∂q u provides the initial guess for each nonlinear solve. In the tested
near-extremal sequences it reaches states at which reusing the previous solution failed to converge.
The predictor is only an initial guess: every retained state must satisfy the same acceptance
checks.

Acceptance and conditioning. An evaluator separate from the generated radial equations
constructs curvature tensors from the metric and checks Gµν + αHµν at 51 off-grid points. We

                                               – 11 –
divide the maximum absolute residual over these points by the maximum reference scale over
the same points. The reference scale is the largest of the Einstein and Gauss–Bonnet tensor
contributions and the square root of the absolute Kretschmann invariant. The relative threshold
is 10−8 ; the absolute residual is recorded as well. The largest relative residual over the accepted
states is 9.76 × 10−9 , and the largest relative Smarr residual is 1.64 × 10−7 . These residuals
diagnose equation closure; they are not bounds on the error of an extracted observable.
   The largest condition number in the selected 10 checks is 5.4 × 107 . Multiplying a condition
number by machine precision estimates possible error amplification, not the number of correct
digits in Ψ. These selected checks do not bound conditioning at all near-extremal states or at
every resolution.


Asymptotic charge extraction. The coefficients in (6) are obtained by fitting compactified
amplitudes in z 2 . The three windows and degrees are z ∈ [0.03, 0.12] at degree 4, z ∈ [0.04, 0.16]
at degree 4, z ∈ [0.03, 0.12] at degree 3. The first is the production choice; the other two measure
sensitivity to that choice. We also compare masses extracted from the b and f asymptotics. The
largest recorded relative diagnostic is 1.31 × 10−8 . All these extractions can share a bias, so a
small spread does not certify their absolute accuracy. At α = 0, comparison with the analytic
M = 3πrH  2 /[8(1 − q 2 )] gives a worst relative deviation 3.27 × 10−13 through q = 0.7. The static

finite-coupling comparison is in section 4.1. We have no corresponding analytic rotating mass at
finite coupling.


Central extrapolation. At each coupling, states are sorted by the physical temperature T
in units rH = 1. The central estimates of M, J, S, Ψ are the intercepts of cubic fits through the
six coldest states. The original degree checks use linear, quadratic and cubic fits through four,
five and six states, respectively. The invariants y, µ, σ are then formed from the extrapolated
charges. The use of unscaled T here is distinct from the use of τ = T J 1/3 to report the distance
to extremality.
