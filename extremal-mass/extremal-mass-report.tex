{\large\bfseries Extremal mass at fixed angular momentum in EGB, $D=5$}\\[1pt]
{\small Status as of 23 October 2026 (previous version: 9 October). Asymptotically flat black holes, two equal
spins $J_1=J_2=J$, $G_5=1$, action $R+\alpha\mathcal L_{\rm GB}$.}

\medskip
We want the smallest mass $M_\ext(J,\alpha)$ that still has a horizon when $J$ is held fixed and the
Gauss--Bonnet coupling $\alpha$ is varied. By dimensional analysis it is a function of one variable:
\[
M_\ext=\pi^{1/3}J^{2/3}\,m(s),\qquad s=\frac{\pi^{2/3}\alpha}{J^{2/3}} .
\]
$s\to0$ is weak coupling (Myers--Perry, $m=3/2$); $s\to\infty$ is strong coupling, or $J\to0$ at fixed $\alpha$.

\textbf{Summary.} We now know $m(s)$ well at both ends and numerically in between:
\[
m(s)=\begin{cases}
\tfrac32+s-\tfrac{236}{105}s^2+d_3s^3+\dots+d_6s^6+\dots, & s\to0 \ (\text{exact coefficients}),\\[3pt]
\tfrac34 s+\tfrac43 s^{-1/2}+a_2s^{-1}+a_3s^{-3/2}+\dots, & s\to\infty,\ \ a_2=0.4566\pm0.0002,\ a_3\simeq-0.263,
\end{cases}
\]
and a ten-term formula (\S4) that reproduces the numerical solution for all $0<s\le103$ to $1\times10^{-5}$.
In physical units the strong-coupling expansion reads
\[
M_\ext=\frac{3\pi\alpha}{4}+\frac{4J}{3\sqrt\alpha}+a_2\,\frac{J^{4/3}}{\pi^{1/3}\alpha}
+a_3\,\frac{J^{5/3}}{\pi^{2/3}\alpha^{3/2}}+\dots
\]
No closed form for $m(s)$ was found, and there are reasons (below) to think none of a simple kind exists.

\textbf{1. Weak coupling: the series through $s^6$.} Same method as before: the exterior metric of the
extremal hole is solved exactly order by order in $\alpha$ (multiple polylogarithms in $1/r^2$), and the mass
is read off at infinity. Two new orders:
\[
d_3=-6.548307,\quad d_4=66.100522,\quad d_5=423.920197004910\ldots,\quad d_6=-4737.494826989936\ldots
\]
(exact forms, with $\zeta(3),\zeta(5),\zeta(3)^2,\pi^{2k}$, in \texttt{series/coefficients.json} and in
\texttt{strong-coupling-report.pdf}). Checks: the extremal first law $dM_\ext=2\Omega_H\,dJ$ holds with zero
residual at orders 2--6, and the horizon radius at orders 5 and 6 matches an independent prediction (next
paragraph) exactly. Same caveat as before: exactness rests on identifying certain horizon constants with
zeta values to 100 digits.

\emph{The series has a small radius of convergence.} The near-horizon geometry of the extremal hole turns
out to be fixed by algebraic equations, so the horizon size is an explicit algebraic function of $s$.
That function has its nearest singularities at $s_*=0.0059\pm0.0548\,i$, i.e.\ $|s_*|=0.055$. The signs of
$d_1,\dots,d_6$ follow the pattern this singularity predicts (6 of 6). Our conclusion, not proved: the weak
series converges only for $|s|\lesssim0.055$ and is useful only as an asymptotic expansion; a resummation
that respects $s_*$ follows the numerical $m(s)$ up to $s\approx0.5$, converging slowly.

\textbf{2. Strong coupling: the leading terms.}
\emph{(a) Where $4/3$ comes from.} We followed the suggestion to use the pure-Lovelock solutions of
Dadhich, Pons and Prabhu [3]. In $D=5$, EGB is the Lovelock theory they call ``derivative-degenerate'', and
its small static black holes ($r\ll\sqrt\alpha$) behave like pure Gauss--Bonnet in its critical dimension,
which has the thermodynamics of a three-dimensional BTZ black hole. We derived the dictionary: AdS radius
$\ell^2=4\alpha$ and $1/(4G_3)=3\pi\alpha$. The static hole with zero horizon radius, of mass $3\pi\alpha/4$,
is the massless BTZ point. For an extremal BTZ hole the mass above that point is
$\Delta M=S^2/(48\pi^3\alpha^2)$ (standard BTZ relation, recalled). Inserting the exact entropy of the
extremal hole from the algebraic near-horizon equations gives $\Delta M\to4J/(3\sqrt\alpha)$, i.e.\
$a_1=4/3$. This is the same coefficient an earlier, independent matched-asymptotics argument had
produced. The step that is assumed rather than derived: the rotating extremal core obeys the static BTZ
dictionary.

\emph{(b) The numbers agree.} We solved the full field equations up to $s=103$ (the earlier numerical branch stopped
at $s=5.24$) and fitted $(m-3s/4)\sqrt s=a_1+a_2s^{-1/2}+a_3s^{-1}+\dots$:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item all coefficients free: $a_1=1.33333$ (fits of different length: $1.3333$--$1.3337$);
\item with $a_1=4/3$ fixed: $a_2=0.45658$--$0.45669$. The earlier matched analysis had predicted
$a_2=0.456527$ under extra assumptions; fixing that value fits the data as well as leaving it free;
\item with $a_1,a_2$ fixed: $a_3$ between $-0.2625$ and $-0.2623$ (with $a_2$ free: $-0.268$ to $-0.265$).
\end{itemize}
So $a_1=4/3$ is confirmed to about $3\times10^{-4}$, $a_2$ is consistent with the predicted value to about $10^{-4}$,
and $a_3$ is a new, purely numerical result.

\textbf{3. The numerical solution.} Up to $s\approx9$ ordinary methods work (shooting from both ends,
checked against the earlier spectral solution). Beyond that, a deformation of the horizon $S^3$ (squashing)
behaves like $e^{\pm4\sqrt6\,\alpha/r}$ inside the core (a factor $e^{76}$ already at $\alpha=10$), so
integrating from one end is hopeless. We used multiple shooting: about 50--130 short segments, matched by
Newton's method, with quadruple precision near the horizon (where the solution has a non-analytic piece
$\propto x^\gamma$, $\gamma$ known exactly) and the large-$r$ series at the outer end. One lesson worth
recording: our first run put the outer end at $25\sqrt\alpha$; there the far-field information is buried
below double-precision noise and the masses came out wrong by up to $10^{-6}$. That run was discarded and
redone at $12\sqrt\alpha$ and $8\sqrt\alpha$, which agree with each other to $5\times10^{-10}$.

\textbf{4. A formula for all $s$.} With $t=(1+s/\kappa)^{-1/2}$ and $\kappa=0.356242695$,
\[
m(s)\approx\frac{3s}{4}+\sum_{k=1}^{10}c_k\,t^k ,
\]
\[
\begin{aligned}
c_{1\dots5}&=2.233910,\ 1.281505,\ -0.713812,\ 6.055449,\ -75.189708,\\
c_{6\dots10}&=230.148117,\ -352.252482,\ 303.906361,\ -142.214324,\ 28.244984.
\end{aligned}
\] It has the right structure at both ends (a Taylor series at $s=0$, a series in
$s^{-1/2}$ at infinity), reproduces $m(0)=3/2$, $m'(0)=1$, $a_1=4/3$, $a_2=0.456527$ by construction, and
matches all 126 numerical points to $1.1\times10^{-5}$ (relative; checked with the printed coefficients).
It is a fit, not the exact function: it misses the singularity at $|s_*|=0.055$, so it should not be
expanded beyond first order in $s$.

\textbf{5. The point $J=0$.} The leading term $3\pi\alpha/4$ is the mass of the static solution with zero
horizon radius, which is singular; the extremal rotating branch ends there, as Kleihaus, Kunz and Radu
conjectured [2]. Our numerics now follow the branch to $J/\alpha^{3/2}=\pi s^{-3/2}\approx0.003$ and show it approaching
this point as the expansion above says.

\textbf{6. What is established and what is not.}
\emph{Exact:} $d_1,\dots,d_6$ (given the zeta identifications); the near-horizon data as algebraic
functions of $s$; the location of $s_*$. \emph{Derived under one assumption:} $a_1=4/3$ (BTZ dictionary
for the rotating core). \emph{Numerical:} $m(s)$ for $0<s\le103$, $a_1$ to $3\times10^{-4}$, $a_2$, $a_3$.
\emph{Not proved:} that $s_*$ is the nearest singularity of $m(s)$ itself; the analytic value of $a_2$.
\emph{Not found:} a closed form. The zeta values in $d_n$ rule out an algebraic $m(s)$ with rational coefficients, and the complex
singularity at $|s|=0.055$ makes an elementary formula built from the two ends unlikely.

\textbf{Possible next steps.} Derive $a_2$ properly (the next order of the core/BTZ matching); push the
numerics to $s\sim200$ (about 6--8 hours with the present code) to pin down $a_3$.
